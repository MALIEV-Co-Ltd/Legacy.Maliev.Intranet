## Anti-Patterns Verdict

### LLM/product-slop test

The interface narrowly passes the obvious “AI-generated dashboard” test. It avoids gradient text, glassmorphism, decorative motion, marketing copy, and ornamental data visualization. It does, however, show template slop: repeated bordered cards, generic Material controls, identical metric-card treatment, and visibly uneven maturity between modules. The dashboard feels intentional; several downstream forms and degraded states feel unfinished.

### Deterministic detector

The detector ran exactly once and exited `2` with findings. The retained output exposed four rules: `overused-font`, `design-system-font`, `design-system-radius`, and `design-system-color`. The stream was too large and was truncated, so an exact finding count is unavailable without violating the one-run constraint.

Most retained detector output needs qualification:

- Inter and Noto Sans Thai are explicitly required by the product design contract, so the generic font warning is not actionable.
- The detector did not parse the prose typography declaration in `DESIGN.md`, producing a false design-system warning.
- Many hits came from generated `obj/Release` scoped-CSS copies and must not be treated as source findings.
- Radius and color literals should be normalized against `design-tokens.css`, but the generic detector output alone does not prove each literal is wrong.

### Overlay and fallback

Browser visualization and DOM inspection worked in a fresh authenticated tab. The available browser evaluation surface was read-only, so the required script-injection preflight and Human overlay could not be performed. No overlay, detector script, or live server was started. Console inspection found no `impeccable` messages, as expected.

## Overall Impression

The migration has a credible employee-operations foundation: a calm visual system, useful dashboard, real permission-scoped data, strong semantic structure, and substantially improved Thai. It is not yet ready to be treated as a dependable desktop-and-mobile work surface. The shell itself breaks at tablet width, dense operational pages are not structurally adapted for mobile, and several workflows terminate in endless loading or generic unavailability.

The experience peaks on the dashboard, where source health, update time, metrics, and recent activity create trust. It falls sharply after navigation into dense queues and details: users can encounter horizontal overflow, 20,000-pixel pages, untranslated contract values, or no terminal state. Error pages have better closure than several business workflows.

## What’s Working

- Strong semantic base: skip link, landmarks, grouped navigation, heading hierarchy, table captions, labeled controls, and textual status cues are consistently present.
- Dashboard information architecture is genuinely operational: source status, permission-aware metrics, recent records, and task-oriented panels establish a useful command center.
- Thai localization is mostly direct and professional rather than machine-literal, especially in permission, recovery, dashboard-health, and system-state copy.
- Desktop render-complete measurements found no document-level horizontal overflow on 42 accepted routes and no duplicate IDs.
- No browser console errors or warnings were captured in the fresh review tab.
- The visual system avoids decorative excess and keeps high-density data legible on desktop.

## Priority Issues

### [P1] Make the shell and dense data views structurally responsive

Evidence:

- Tablet pages had a 758 px client width but up to 873 px document width because the employee profile control extended beyond the viewport.
- Mobile dashboard tables measured roughly 608 px and 503 px inside a 380 px viewport.
- Mobile order detail reached 400 px against a 380 px client width.
- Mobile `/Orders/Index` rendered roughly 20,151 px of vertical content.

Why it matters: a global tablet scrollbar damages every workflow; mobile users must discover nested scrolling or traverse pages too long for practical queue work.

Concrete fix: collapse identity to avatar/menu before 768 px; use responsive record cards or priority-column tables; paginate or virtualize long queues; keep filters and primary actions sticky; and enforce zero page-level overflow after the Blazor route finishes rendering.

Suggested command: `$impeccable adapt`

### [P1] Give every loading or degraded state a terminal outcome

Evidence:

- Finance, invoice, material, purchase-order, employee, quotation, and quotation-request details repeatedly remained at `กำลังโหลด…`.
- Quotation details exposed temporary unavailability without retry or diagnostic guidance.
- Permission-denied states were clearer but often appeared only after entering a module.

Why it matters: employees cannot distinguish a slow request, a missing record, an authorization loss, or a failed dependency.

Concrete fix: time-bound loaders; preserve the requested record ID; transition to module-specific unavailable/not-found/forbidden states; offer retry, back, and report actions; and expose service/permission availability before deep navigation where possible.

Suggested command: `$impeccable harden`

### [P1] Localize and format boundary values before display

Evidence:

- `MaterialId_Descending` appeared as a sort label.
- Order history exposed English states such as `Paid`, `Accepted`, `Quoted`, `Reviewed`, `Reviewing`, and `New` in Thai UI.
- Raw timestamps such as `2026-07-10 10:06:14Z` and ISO employee dates were displayed.
- The Thai primary navigation still labels the route `Dashboard`.

Why it matters: contract leakage reduces trust and raw UTC can cause operational mistakes. Stored timestamps should remain UTC, while employee display must consistently use Asia/Bangkok and the agreed Thai date convention.

Concrete fix: centralize enum/sort/status labels; convert UTC to Asia/Bangkok at the presentation boundary; choose one consistent Thai calendar/date-time format; and add localization snapshot/contract tests for every exposed enum and date field.

Suggested command: `$impeccable clarify`

### [P1] Decompose high-risk forms and preserve task context

Evidence:

- Purchase-order creation exposes 25 controls in a roughly 2,717 px mobile page.
- Supplier editing exposes 17 controls and shares the surface with deletion.
- Order detail combines about 26 controls/actions across roughly 4,523 px, including commercial edits, status changes, history, and files.
- Numeric steppers measured about 24 × 28 px, below the 44 × 44 target.
- Some supplier/address selectors visibly defaulted to `0`.

Why it matters: users retain too much context across several screens and can edit the wrong section or trigger a high-impact action unintentionally.

Concrete fix: introduce progressive sections with summaries; separate order overview, commercial edits, workflow status, and files; keep record identity and financial summary visible; replace `0` with meaningful empty/default states; enlarge touch targets; and isolate destructive actions.

Suggested command: `$impeccable distill`

### [P1] Make the mobile navigation drawer a real accessible modal

Evidence:

- Focus remained on the background opener after the drawer opened rather than moving to the close control.
- The underlying main content remained operable and was not inert.
- Escape closed the drawer, but explicit focus restoration was not demonstrated.
- Several text links and number controls had interaction boxes below 44 px.

Why it matters: keyboard and assistive-technology users can enter visually obscured background content, while touch users face undersized controls.

Concrete fix: move focus into the drawer; trap it; mark the workspace inert/hidden while open; restore focus to the opener on close; and give every mobile navigation/action target a minimum 44 px hit box.

Suggested command: `$impeccable audit`

## Cognitive Load

Five of eight product cognitive-load checks fail. Related information is generally grouped, page-title hierarchy is clear, and desktop panels are recognizable. The failures are single focus, limited choices, one task at a time, working-memory support, and progressive disclosure.

Decision-heavy surfaces include the 24+ route navigation, six dashboard workspaces, five finance categories, the 25-control purchase-order form, and multi-action order detail. Mobile page lengths of approximately 20,151 px, 4,523 px, and 2,717 px are evidence that desktop density was stacked rather than redesigned.

## Persona Red Flags

- **Alex — power user:** no visible keyboard shortcuts, saved filters, bulk actions, or compact expert view. The mobile order queue makes rapid scanning impractical.
- **Sam — accessibility-dependent:** drawer focus stays outside the open drawer, the background remains operable, and several links/steppers are below the 44 px interaction target.
- **Casey — distracted mobile employee:** primary actions may be thousands of pixels below the title; long forms have no persistent summary or staged progress; repeated address fields are interruption-hostile.
- **Riley — operations administrator:** unbounded loaders and generic unavailable states provide too little diagnostic context to distinguish data, permission, or dependency problems.

## Minor Observations

- Authenticated `/` still invites the user to sign in, contradicting the authenticated shell.
- Desktop dashboard composition leaves substantial unused space while dense panels remain constrained.
- Metric cards are consistent but overly repetitive and visually generic.
- The tablet top bar truncates employee identity and widens the entire document.
- The mobile quick action becomes a bare `+`; it has an accessible name, but its visible purpose is ambiguous.
- Dashboard dates use a Thai Buddhist-era treatment while several detail pages use Gregorian ISO/UTC formatting.
- Status colors are paired with text, which is a good accessibility choice.
- Generic DOM heuristics flagged possible unnamed MudBlazor-generated inputs/buttons on several forms; these are candidates for an accessibility-tree audit, not confirmed failures.

## Questions to Consider

1. Is mobile primarily for approvals and quick operational checks, or must it expose the full desktop editing surface? The answer determines whether to build an approval-first mobile mode or fully recompose every form.
2. Which four fields determine the next action on an order? Those should remain visible while status, commercial edits, files, and history become separate task surfaces.
3. Should unavailable or unauthorized modules remain fully navigable, or should the shell expose capability/service availability before employees enter them?
